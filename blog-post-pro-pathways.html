Player Pathways • College Recruiting

If your 16-year-old isn’t in an MLS academy, it can feel like the door to professional soccer just closed. It hasn’t. It just means you’re looking at a different set of doors — and most parents have no idea those doors even exist, let alone which one to knock on first, what it costs, when it starts, or what it does to college eligibility.

This guide covers the whole pathway, top to bottom: pro academies, MLS NEXT, USL Academy, UPSL, NPSL, and USL League Two. For each one you’ll get who plays, when the season runs, what it actually costs, the rules that affect your kid, and how it touches NCAA eligibility — plus a full FAQ for the questions that come up over and over in our community.

We’re starting at the top with the pro academies, because that’s where most families get the story wrong before they get anywhere else.

Pro Academies: What Making It Actually Means

There are two genuinely professional academy systems for boys in this country: MLS club academies (roughly 30 of them, one per MLS club) and the academies run by USL Championship and USL League One clubs. Everything else calling itself an “academy” is a youth club using the word as marketing.

The distinction that matters isn’t the badge. It’s who pays. A real pro academy is funded by the professional club — no player fees, gear provided, travel covered, and for residency players often housing and schooling as well. If a program is charging your family thousands of dollars a year, it is not a pro academy, whatever the website says.

Why clubs fund it: the Homegrown rule

MLS academies exist for one commercial reason. Under the Homegrown Player rule, an MLS club can sign a player who came through its own academy directly to the first team — no draft, no competition from the other 29 clubs. The academy is how a club manufactures its own talent instead of buying it.

Below the first team sits MLS NEXT Pro, launched in 2022 as a third-division league of MLS reserve sides plus independent clubs. It’s where academy graduates get real professional minutes before — or instead of — breaking into the first team. That’s the full ladder: academy → MLS NEXT Pro → first team.

The Honest Timing Problem

Most academy places are filled at U13 to U15. By 16, a pro academy is generally not looking to develop a raw player — it’s looking to fill a specific positional gap with someone who can contribute immediately. It absolutely still happens. It just isn’t the volume game it was three years earlier, and no amount of emailing changes that.

Worth saying plainly: “free” is real, but the commitment isn’t free. Four to five sessions a week, significant travel, and for a lot of families either a brutal commute or an actual relocation. Before you chase it, be honest about whether your household can absorb that — and ask the hard questions of any program, pro or not.

Inside an MLS Academy: Age Divisions and the Homegrown Rule

Once a family realizes MLS academies are genuinely free, two questions follow immediately: what ages do they even take? and what actually happens if my kid makes it? Almost nobody answers either one plainly, so here they are.

Part 1: how the age divisions work

An MLS academy doesn’t field one team. It fields a stack of them, one per age division, and those divisions line up with MLS NEXT’s structure: U13, U14, U15, U16, U17 and U19. A number of clubs also run a pre-academy or U12 program underneath as a feeder, which is where a lot of the real identification quietly happens.

Understanding that stack tells you where the doors actually are. U13 is the big intake year — that’s when a club builds a group. U15 is the second real window, when bodies change and clubs reassess. After that, movement is mostly replacement: someone leaves, gets injured, or ages out, and a club fills that exact hole. It’s not that a 16-year-old can’t get in. It’s that he’s competing for a vacancy rather than a place in a new group.

Two more structural things worth knowing. MLS NEXT groups clubs into regional divisions and conferences for the regular season, then brings the best together for a national playoff and for showcase events — and those showcases are where college and pro scouts actually congregate, far more than at any regular-season fixture. And clubs differ enormously on residency versus commuting: a handful relocate players and school them on site, while most academy players live at home and travel in. Ask which model a club runs before your family falls in love with the badge.

Part 2: what Homegrown actually means

This is the mechanism the whole system is built around, and it’s worth understanding before you commit years of your life to it.

Since 2008, an MLS club has been able to sign a player who came through its own academy directly to its first team — no SuperDraft, no other 29 clubs bidding. That’s the Homegrown Player rule, and it is the entire commercial reason a professional club pays for your kid’s coaching, kit and travel.

Homegrown territory

Each MLS club holds a defined geographic territory. A player from inside it who joins that academy generally becomes that club’s homegrown right. This is exactly why which club is nearest is not a trivial detail — geography assigns who can claim your son.

There's a time requirement

A player has to have been registered with the academy for a minimum period before he can be signed as a Homegrown. It is not a one-session arrangement, and the specifics move with the collective bargaining agreement — so confirm the current rule with the club, not with a forum post.

Clubs get a roster advantage

Homegrown signings carry roster and salary-budget benefits versus a comparable outside signing. That’s deliberate — MLS designed the incentive so clubs would rather develop a player than buy one.

And a resale line

Academy products sold on to European clubs have become a genuine revenue stream for MLS sides. Your son isn’t only a squad player in that model — he’s a potential asset, which cuts both ways.

The Fork In The Road

A Homegrown contract is a professional contract. Signing it ends NCAA eligibility permanently. That decision typically lands at 17 or 18, it has real money attached, and it is not reversible — so it deserves a conversation in your household long before anyone puts a pen in your son’s hand. Playing in the academy costs you nothing in eligibility terms. Signing does.

What to actually ask an MLS academy

  • ✓Is my son inside your homegrown territory — and if not, what does that change?
  • ✓How long must he be registered here before he could be signed as a Homegrown?
  • ✓How many Homegrown players have you actually signed in the last five years — and how many of them got real minutes?
  • ✓Do you run a residency program, and is my son expected to relocate?
  • ✓What happens to a player you release at U16 — where do those boys typically land?
  • ✓How does your college advising work for the majority who won’t sign?

That last question is the one that separates a serious program from a hopeful one. The overwhelming majority of academy players — including at MLS clubs — do not sign a professional contract. A program that has thought carefully about what happens to those boys is a program worth joining. One that hasn’t is selling you a lottery ticket. MLS NEXT: Most of It Isn't MLS

This is the single most useful thing in this article, so here it is on its own line:

Not making an MLS academy does not mean MLS NEXT is closed to your kid. Roughly 150 clubs play in MLS NEXT. Only about 30 of them are MLS academies.

What it is: MLS NEXT launched in 2020, stepping into the hole left when U.S. Soccer shut down its Development Academy. It’s the top competitive youth platform in the country, running age groups from U13 through U19, and it’s operated by MLS — which is exactly why the name confuses people.

The MLS club academies play in it. So do well over a hundred independent elite clubs with no MLS affiliation whatsoever. Those independent clubs are where the overwhelming majority of MLS NEXT roster spots actually are — and they are reachable by a strong 16-year-old in a way an MLS academy usually isn’t.

Season: The academic year, roughly September through June or July, finishing at MLS NEXT Cup. That’s a genuine structural difference from everything else in this guide — MLS NEXT is the pathway that runs during school. UPSL, NPSL, and USL2 are built around the summer.

The catch is the invoice

MLS academies are free. The independent MLS NEXT clubs are emphatically not. They charge full competitive club fees, and once you add national travel to that number, MLS NEXT at a non-MLS club sits among the most expensive youth soccer in America. Same league, same badge, wildly different bill depending on which of the 150 clubs your kid signs with.

USL Academy: The Newest and Most Direct Pipeline

What it is: A club-run academy system launched in 2021, connecting directly into USL Championship, USL League One, and USL League Two clubs. For 2026, the league grew to 90 clubs across 15 men’s divisions — a big jump that included 28 new teams, five of them from USL’s professional ranks and nine from the pre-professional side.

Clubs to know: New Mexico United, Phoenix Rising FC, El Paso Locomotive FC, FC Tucson, Charlotte Eagles, Charlotte Independence, SC United Bantams, Greenville Triumph, and South Georgia Tormenta FC — largely mirroring USL’s professional footprint, plus a mix of independent academies like Barca Residency Academy competing alongside the club-affiliated programs.

Season: Timing varies by club and division rather than one fixed league-wide start date. As an example, Tormenta FC’s 2026 Southeast Division schedule ran an 8-game slate from late May through July against regional opponents like Charlotte Eagles and Charlotte Independence. Expect similar late-spring-through-summer windows across most divisions, but always confirm with the specific club.

The rules that actually affect your kid

One squad, not age groups

Instead of separate U15/U17/U20 teams like a traditional club academy, each USL Academy team is a single squad spanning U20 and below, with a required minimum number of U17-and-younger players to keep the age balance honest.

Five over-age players on gameday

Teams can dress up to five over-age players, which means a strong 16-year-old regularly competes alongside 19- and 20-year-olds instead of against his own birth year.

USL Academy Contracts

The standout feature. These let a player train — and even appear in matches — with a USL senior pro team while preserving college eligibility, provided they meet NCAA amateurism requirements.

Mandatory college advising

Every USL Academy club is required to run a college advisory program — USL openly acknowledges most players in the system will still go the college route.

What It Costs

Individual club academies — New Mexico United is the clearest public example — are advertised as free for selected players, covering coaching, competition, uniform, and travel/lodging/meals during academy play. This isn’t a league-wide mandate the way it is in MLS Next, so confirm with the specific club, but the flagship model is tuition-free.

One practical thing that trips families up when they move between these systems: registration and player cards. Different leagues, different governing bodies, different paperwork — and a missed card is a missed tournament.

UPSL: The Biggest Net, But Not a Youth Development League

What it is: UPSL is an American for-profit adult soccer league founded in 2011 in Santa Ana, California. It has grown into the largest pro-development league in North America, with 900+ men’s and women’s clubs, and is widely considered the unofficial 4th tier of U.S. Soccer — sitting just below MLS, USL Championship, and USL League One.

Clubs to know (2025–26): Deportivo Rose City (2026 Spring National Champion), Miami United FC, New York Renegades FC (2025 Fall National Champion), and California United FC II — one of the winningest clubs in league history. UPSL has also become a notable launchpad for international talent; Yan Diomande, a former UPSL National Finals MVP, went on to sign with Real Madrid after a stint at RB Leipzig — the kind of story UPSL leans on heavily in its marketing.

The Calendar Difference

UPSL runs two seasons a year: Spring starts in March and runs to national playoffs in late July/early August; Fall starts in September and runs to national playoffs in early February. That year-round structure is UPSL’s biggest practical difference from NPSL and USL League Two, which are essentially summer-only.

The rules that actually affect your kid

  • ●No official minimum player age — but UPSL is fundamentally an adult league. Average participant age is 22.5. Because minors on a roster trigger SafeSport child-safety training and background-check requirements plus locker-room separation rules, many clubs simply keep rosters 18+.
  • ●Each club is independently owned and operated — so pay structures, coaching quality, and how seriously a club treats development vary enormously from one club to the next, even inside the same conference.
  • ●Internal promotion and relegation exists between the Premier Division and Division I in most conferences, and some regions (historically SoCal and Colorado) run an additional Championship Division tier below Premier.
  • ●The Premier Division alone had 344 clubs competing for 32 national playoff spots in Spring 2026. This is a genuinely large, competitive ecosystem — not a niche one.

If your player is in the gap between youth club and adult soccer, guest playing is the underrated way to keep getting competitive minutes while you sort the longer-term plan out.

NPSL: The Amateur Alternative

What it is: The National Premier Soccer League is UPSL’s closest direct competitor, occupying the same unofficial 4th tier. For 2026, 55 teams compete across 8 conferences.

Season: The 2026 regular season ran May 2 – July 4 — a tighter, summer-focused window compared to UPSL’s year-round two-season model.

  • ●NPSL is explicitly structured to accommodate college players in the off-season, similar in spirit to USL League Two — though its amateurism rules have historically been looser and more club-dependent than USL2’s.
  • ●The league has seen real churn recently — several clubs left NPSL in 2026 for USL League Two, NISA, or newer regional alternatives like the Florida Champions League and The League for Clubs (a 2024-founded amateur league with backing from figures like Chicago Fire GM Peter Wilt).
  • ●Because it’s built around a shorter summer window, it lines up more naturally with a college player’s off-season than with a 16-year-old’s academic-year schedule.

USL League Two: The College Showcase League

What it is: The oldest of the fourth-tier leagues, operating since 1995 (formerly the PDL). For 2026 it’s the largest of the summer leagues by far — 158 teams across 20 divisions in Central, Eastern, Southern, and Western conferences.

Season: The 2026 regular season ran May 3 – July 12, with playoffs July 13 – August 1 — almost identical timing to NPSL, since both exist to fill the same college off-season window.

  • ●USL2 was built from day one to protect NCAA eligibility. It functions as a “pre-professional” rather than professional league specifically so college players can compete without burning their amateur status.
  • ●Roster rules limit teams to eight players over the age of 23, keeping the league genuinely youth- and college-age-weighted rather than drifting into an adult men’s league the way UPSL can.
  • ●Players may be reimbursed for expenses without it counting against amateur status, but the league does not pay salaries to its predominantly amateur player pool.

The summer leagues all run May through July for the same reason — which means the months before May are when a player either arrives ready or arrives behind. That window is entirely in your family’s control.

How This Affects College (NCAA) Eligibility

This is the question that trips up more families than anything else, so let’s be precise.

The Core Rule

A player loses amateur status if they use their athletic skill for pay, sign any kind of professional contract, accept a promise of future pay, or compete on a team that declares itself professional — regardless of whether the player personally received money.

Safe

MLS NEXT & Pro Academies

Fully amateur, and the most heavily scouted youth environment in the country. Playing here costs you nothing in eligibility terms.

Safest

USL League Two

Purpose-built around the amateurism rule. It’s why so many current NCAA athletes play there every summer.

Check First

NPSL

Same spirit, more variation. The protections are real but historically less airtight than USL2’s — confirm a specific club’s structure before committing.

Real Diligence

UPSL

Pay decisions are left to each independently-owned club. If a club pays players — even informally — participating can jeopardize amateur status even for a player who received nothing.

On top of the pay question, NCAA rules generally block a student-athlete from competing for an outside team during their college sport’s declared season — so timing matters as much as pay structure. Always confirm directly with a specific UPSL club how they operate before a player who wants to preserve college eligibility joins.

What Actually Ends It

Signing a Homegrown contract with an MLS club, or an MLS NEXT Pro contract, is signing a professional contract. That ends NCAA eligibility, full stop — and it’s a genuine fork in the road, not a technicality. Note the distinction: MLS clubs can field academy players in MLS NEXT Pro matches as amateurs without signing them. It’s the signature that changes everything, not the appearance.

The Interesting Middle Ground

USL Academy Contracts explicitly let a player train and even appear in matches with a USL senior professional team while preserving college eligibility, as long as the arrangement stays inside what the NCAA permits (no more than actual and necessary expenses, no employment-style pay). It’s one of the more parent-friendly innovations in the current landscape — real professional-environment exposure without forcing an early, irreversible choice between college and pro.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: the NCAA’s “May 1 exception” generally prohibits college soccer players from competing outside their school before May 1 of a given year — which is part of why the NPSL, USL2, and UPSL summer seasons all cluster in that May–July window. They’re built around that rule, not in spite of it.

Parent FAQ

We didn’t get into an MLS academy. Is MLS NEXT still possible?

Yes — and this is the misunderstanding that costs families the most. Roughly 150 clubs play in MLS NEXT and only about 30 are MLS academies. The other ~120 are independent elite clubs that recruit openly. Missing an MLS academy closes one door out of roughly 150.

Is MLS NEXT free?

Only if it’s an MLS club academy. Those are fully funded. The independent MLS NEXT clubs charge full competitive club fees plus national travel, which puts them among the most expensive youth soccer in the country. Same league, completely different bill.

Can a 16-year-old still get into a pro academy?

It happens, but understand the odds. Most academy places are filled at U13–U15, and by 16 a club is typically filling a specific positional gap rather than developing a raw player. Treat it as a possible outcome of playing well somewhere visible, not as a plan in itself.

My 16-year-old isn’t in a pro academy. What should we actually do first?

Be honest about physical and tactical readiness for adult or near-adult competition. If you can carry the cost, a non-MLS MLS NEXT club is the best-scouted room in the country. If you can’t and you’re near a USL market, USL Academy is the strongest free option and preserves every future choice. If neither fits, keep building in a strong club environment for another year before pushing into UPSL or NPSL.

Can a 16-year-old play in UPSL?

Technically yes — there’s no official minimum age — but it’s uncommon. UPSL is an adult league (average age 22.5), and many clubs keep rosters 18+ specifically to avoid the SafeSport and liability complications that come with minors on an adult roster.

Is UPSL the best league for a 16-year-old trying to go pro outside an academy?

No single league is definitively “best.” UPSL is genuinely valuable, but usually a year or two later than most parents assume — it’s a competitive outlet for players 17+, not a youth development curriculum.

Does playing UPSL kill college eligibility?

Not automatically — but it can, depending entirely on how the specific club operates. Because clubs are independently owned and UPSL doesn’t mandate amateur status league-wide, always verify with the club directly before a college-track player joins.

What actually ends NCAA eligibility?

Signing a professional contract — a Homegrown deal with an MLS club, an MLS NEXT Pro contract, or any paid arrangement with a club that declares itself professional. Playing MLS NEXT, a pro academy, USL Academy, or USL League Two does not.

What’s the actual difference between NPSL and USL League Two?

Both run almost the same summer window (roughly May–July) and both exist mainly to give college players a competitive off-season outlet. USL2 is larger, older, and has stricter built-in NCAA protections; NPSL is a comparable but somewhat smaller and more club-dependent alternative — sometimes the better local option depending on which league has stronger clubs in your region.

Is USL Academy actually free?

The flagship examples (like New Mexico United) are free for selected players, covering coaching, competition, uniforms, and travel. It’s not a league-wide guarantee the way it is with MLS academies, so confirm funding directly with the specific club.

When do these seasons actually start?

  • Pro academies & MLS NEXT: the school year, roughly September–July
  • USL Academy: varies by club, generally late spring through summer
  • UPSL: two seasons — Spring (March–August) and Fall (September–February)
  • NPSL: May–July
  • USL League Two: May–August (regular season through playoffs)

What if my player wants to preserve college eligibility and get pro-level exposure?

Look hard at a USL Academy Contract if it’s geographically available. It’s currently the clearest pathway on this list explicitly designed to let a player train and play with a senior professional team while keeping NCAA eligibility fully intact.

The Bottom Line

Not making an MLS academy closes exactly one door. Here’s the honest ranking of what’s left.

If you can carry the cost, a non-MLS MLS NEXT club is the strongest college-recruiting environment in the country, and it’s open to a strong 16-year-old in a way an MLS academy usually isn’t. If you can’t, USL Academy — where geographically available — is the clearest, most direct, and most cost-free pathway toward a professional opportunity while preserving every future option.

UPSL is a real and valuable tool, but it’s an adult competitive league that becomes most useful a year or two later than most parents assume, and it demands real diligence around pay structure if college eligibility matters to your family. NPSL and USL League Two are the safer, NCAA-protected summer options once your player is closer to college age.

“The families who navigate this well aren’t the ones who found the ‘best’ league. They’re the ones who got honest about where their kid actually is right now, checked the eligibility fine print before committing, and picked the environment that matched — not the one with the most buzz in the group chat.”

And whichever door your family ends up knocking on, the thing that decides whether it opens is the same one it’s always been: what your player does on the ball between now and then. Every league on this list is scouting for the same thing.
